## 2. Retrieval Architectures: BM25, Dense Embeddings, And Hybrid Fusion

### 2.1 Sparse BM25 Versus Dense Semantic Retrieval On Long Technical Documents

A central question for your RAG pipeline is how to retrieve relevant passages from a large corpus of scientific PDFs. Classical information retrieval uses sparse, lexical methods like BM25, which score documents based on term frequency and inverse document frequency with length normalization, while modern dense retrievers map queries and documents to vectors via neural embedding models and perform nearest‑neighbor search.[10][19] Recent benchmarks in financial RAG and scientific retrieval demonstrate that dense methods are not universally superior: BM25 often outperforms state‑of‑the‑art dense retrieval on long, technical documents, particularly in zero‑shot or out‑of‑domain settings.[10][6] 

In the “From BM25 to Corrective RAG” benchmark, researchers evaluated ten retrieval strategies on T²‑RAGBench, a financial corpus comprising 7,318 text‑and‑table documents and over 23,000 queries. They encoded all queries and documents with a strong dense embedding model (OpenAI text‑embedding‑3‑large) and indexed them with FAISS, while sparse retrieval used Okapi BM25 with tuned parameters.[10] The results showed that BM25 achieved higher recall and ranking metrics than dense retrieval on most measures, with BM25’s Recall@5 around 0.644 compared to 0.587 for dense retrieval, and similar trends across Recall@1, Recall@3, and mean reciprocal rank.[10] Only at higher cutoffs, such as Recall@20, did dense retrieval slightly edge BM25. This performance gap surprised many practitioners who had assumed semantic search would dominate; the authors attribute BM25’s advantage to its lexical precision, especially for exact term matching of financial numerical expressions and domain‑specific terminology.[10] 

Scientific retrieval tasks echo this finding. Ma et al. showed that hybrid sparse–dense search significantly improves DPR’s retrieval accuracy on Wikipedia QA, but also that BM25 alone is remarkably effective and space‑efficient, with dense passage retrieval’s flat index over Wikipedia occupying about 61 GB compared to 2.4 GB for BM25’s inverted index.[19] In SciRerankBench, a benchmark explicitly focused on scientific reranking, dense passage retrieval is used as a first‑stage retriever, but the study highlights that the choice of reranking architecture is more critical than the dense retriever itself, implying that sparse methods could also serve as effective first stages.[6] Together, these results suggest that for your domain of accelerator physics and AI papers—where exact terminology, formula names, and acronyms are common—BM25 should be considered a competitive baseline rather than merely a fallback. It will likely outperform naive dense retrieval for precise numerical and terminological queries, aligning with the financial RAG finding that BM25 beats dense retrieval on text‑and‑table corpora.[10][19]

### 2.2 Hybrid BM25+Dense Retrieval With Reciprocal Rank Fusion

The strongest retrieval performance on T²‑RAGBench comes not from BM25 alone but from hybrid sparse‑dense fusion followed by neural reranking. In the financial RAG benchmark, a two‑stage pipeline combining hybrid retrieval via Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) with a Cohere cross‑encoder reranker achieved Recall@5 of approximately 0.816 and MRR@3 around 0.605, clearly outperforming all single‑stage methods, including BM25, dense retrieval, and hybrid fusion without reranking.[10] Hybrid retrieval itself, using BM25 and dense embeddings fused through RRF, improves recall by 15–30% over single‑method pipelines, yielding Recall@5 around 0.695, Recall@10 around 0.801, and correspondingly higher nDCG and MAP scores.[10] Specifically, the hybrid RRF method beats BM25 and dense retrieval at nearly all cutoffs, demonstrating that combining lexical and semantic signals captures more relevant documents than either alone.[10] 

RRF is particularly attractive because it is unsupervised and does not require score normalization: it fuses ranked lists by summing reciprocal ranks, giving more weight to high‑ranking items in each list, and has been shown to consistently outperform alternative fusion strategies such as Condorcet voting and CombMNZ.[10][19] In Ma et al.’s sparse–dense hybrid search for DPR, a similar fusion strategy yielded improved retrieval accuracy with substantial compression, illustrating that simple rank‑based combinations of BM25 and dense search can maintain or enhance performance even when dense indices are compressed.[19] For your competition, where you may generate dense embeddings using Olmo and perform BM25 with classical tooling, RRF offers a low‑cost, high‑impact way to fuse these signals without training additional models. You can compute BM25 scores and dense retrieval ranks for each query, then apply RRF to obtain a single fused ranking from which you select top candidates for chunking and reranking.

It is important to recognize, however, that hybrid retrieval’s benefits are domain‑dependent. The financial RAG benchmark shows that hybrid+RRF improves retrieval metrics across the board, but query expansion methods like HyDE and multi‑query retrieval provide limited benefit for precise numerical questions, suggesting that hybrid retrieval’s strength lies in covering semantic diversity rather than resolving numerical ambiguity.[10] In scientific QA over accelerator physics and AI, many questions will likely mix qualitative reasoning and quantitative specification. Hybrid BM25+dense with RRF will be powerful for locating conceptually relevant sections, definitions, and theoretical discussions, while BM25 alone may suffice—or even outperform—for strictly numeric or parameter‑specific queries, echoing the finding that BM25 remains competitive for precise financial quantities.[10] 

### 2.3 Document‑Level Routing With Mixture Of Document Experts (MODE)

When the corpus grows very large and heterogeneous, even hybrid retrieval over individual chunks can be inefficient and noisy. The MODE framework (“Mixture of Document Experts for RAG”) introduces a different paradigm: instead of searching over individual items, it organizes documents into semantically coherent clusters, each serving as a “document expert,” and routes queries to the most relevant cluster for context retrieval.[14] MODE adapts the mixture‑of‑experts idea to the data level rather than the model level. During preprocessing, documents are embedded and clustered into M groups, each representing a thematic topic. At inference time, the query embedding is compared against these cached centroids using cosine similarity, and the top m clusters (typically one or two) are selected. Retrieval then proceeds within these clusters, drastically narrowing the search space while leveraging specialization at the cluster level.[14] 

The advantage of document‑expert routing is twofold. First, it improves retrieval efficiency by avoiding full‑corpus search for every query: queries are routed to a small subset of clusters, within which dense and sparse search can be performed more quickly.[14] Second, it can enhance retrieval precision by aligning queries with clusters that represent distinct subdomains, thereby reducing cross‑domain interference and distractor retrieval. In your competition, where the corpus combines accelerator physics IFMIF‑DONES reports with AI papers and distractors, MODE‑style clustering could separate physics reports from AI research, and further subdivide each into thematic experts, such as experimental design, simulation studies, safety assessments, and algorithmic innovations.[14] Queries about neutron flux or target design would route to the relevant accelerator cluster, whereas questions about transformer architectures or optimization methods would route to AI clusters, ensuring that retrieval focuses on the correct domain. This is analogous to the way MODE improves RAG by routing queries to document experts, mitigating the risk of irrelevant contexts.[14] 

Implementing MODE with Olmo as your only model requires you to use Olmo‑generated embeddings for both documents and queries, then perform clustering and centroid storage offline. Olmo‑3.1’s instruction‑tuned nature should not impede its ability to produce reasonable embeddings when prompted appropriately, although you may need to design a specific embedding prompt to ensure that the representation captures document topics rather than surface lexicon.[16] Once clusters are established, BM25 and dense retrieval can be combined within each expert, and RRF can be applied to fuse their rankings, building a multi‑stage pipeline: cluster routing, hybrid retrieval within cluster, and Olmo‑based reranking. The MODE paper does not directly report retrieval metric numbers in the excerpt, but its conceptual contribution—routing by document experts—addresses the challenge you face with mixed domain PDFs and distractors, suggesting that clustering the corpus into physics and AI experts is particularly valuable.[14]

### 2.4 Parent‑Document Retrieval Patterns And Context Relevance

An increasingly recognized pitfall in RAG systems is the parent‑document retriever pattern: the system searches over small child chunks but returns the larger parent document or section that contains the match as context to the generator. The FutureAGI description of parent‑document retrievers explains that this pattern often arises when retrievers are optimized for child chunks but the downstream application requests the full parent section, for instance to provide “more context” or to avoid cutting off relevant information.[17] The symptoms include high vector hit rates, relatively flat reranker scores, rising prompt token counts, lower measured context relevance, and increased groundedness failures on long parent passages.[17] Essentially, the system retrieves many large parent contexts that contain the correct child chunk somewhere inside, but the generator uses only a small fraction of the passage, leading to wasted tokens and reduced answer quality.

To diagnose and mitigate parent‑document retriever issues, FutureAGI recommends storing both child and parent IDs and evaluating retrieval spans with metrics like ChunkAttribution, ContextRelevance, and ChunkUtilization. ChunkAttribution checks whether the answer references retrieved evidence; ContextRelevance assesses whether the parent context was useful for the query; and ChunkUtilization flags parents whose retrieved text was mostly unused.[17] These metrics allow you to identify when parent contexts are too large or poorly aligned, causing the generator to hallucinate or ignore the evidence. For your competition, where Olmo has a finite context window and must answer multiple questions within an hour, parent‑document retrievers are a serious risk. Scientific PDFs often have long sections with only a few lines relevant to a given question; if you feed entire sections into the prompt, you will reduce the number of questions you can answer and increase the chance of off‑topic or contradictory information, especially when distractor documents are present.[17] 

The recommended alternative is to design chunking and retrieval pipelines that return smaller, focused contexts with contextual headers rather than large parent passages. Vision‑guided chunking for multimodal documents, for example, uses segmentation methods that respect visual layout and section boundaries, yielding chunks that are semantically coherent while not overly long.[11] Similarly, contextual retrieval in Corrective RAG enriches document embeddings with metadata and surrounding paragraphs without ballooning the size of individual chunks, improving retrieval performance by roughly 15–30% in recall over basic dense retrieval.[10] By combining these approaches, you can maintain high context relevance while avoiding the parent‑document retriever pattern: search over child chunks with enriched headers, return only those chunks or small groups of contiguous chunks, and limit parent context length to what Olmo can reasonably process, tracking ChunkUtilization to verify that retrieved text is actually used.[10][17][11] This strategy aligns well with your time and token budget, maximizing the impact of each retrieved passage.

## 3. Chunking, Context Windows, And Long‑Context Versus RAG Trade‑Offs

### 3.1 Chunk Granularity, Contextual Headers, And Vision‑Guided Chunking

Chunking—how you split PDFs into retrievable units—is one of the most important and underappreciated design choices in a RAG system. Recent work on multimodal chunking, such as “Vision‑Guided Chunking Is All You Need,” shows that chunking strategies significantly influence downstream RAG performance, particularly when documents contain figures, tables, and complex layouts.[11] In that study, the authors develop a new benchmark for multimodal document chunking evaluation and validate their approach using Gemini‑2.5‑Pro as the chunking model and GPT‑4.1 as the summarization model on diverse document types. They then assess RAG performance downstream, demonstrating that vision‑guided chunking, which aligns chunks with visual and structural cues like section headers, figure captions, and table boundaries, leads to more relevant and coherent contexts.[11] 

Although your competition’s PDFs are primarily scientific, they likely contain many tables and figures whose content is critical for answering expert questions. If chunks are defined purely by fixed token windows—say, every 512 tokens—the model may split tables across chunks or mix distinct sections, reducing retrieval precision and making it harder for the generator to construct a coherent answer.[11] Vision‑guided chunking strategies suggest that you should respect document structure: treat each section, table, figure, and equation block as a potential chunk, with contextual headers that capture the section title, document title, and key metadata. These headers can be included in the text fed to the retriever, ensuring that BM25 and dense search can match queries to section names and figure titles, while the body of the chunk contains the detailed content.[11][10] Corrective RAG’s notion of contextual retrieval similarly emphasizes enriching document representations with context, such as including table headers and section summaries in the embedding, which helps dense retrieval identify relevant passages even when the query uses different phrasing than the source content.[10] 

For your pipeline, a practical approach is to parse PDFs into sections and tables, generate short textual headers for each chunk that include the document title, section title, and high‑level description, and append these headers to the chunk text for retrieval. BM25 will leverage the headers for exact keyword matching, while Olmo‑based embeddings can capture semantic similarity between queries and section descriptions.[10][11] Detailed tables can be extracted into structured representations and associated with short textual summaries that describe the type of data (e.g., “neutron flux versus radius for IFMIF target” or “hyperparameter grid for transformer training”), enabling query matching without overloading chunks with raw numerical content.[4][10] This chunk‑plus‑header design directly addresses the parent‑document retriever problem by keeping chunks small but context‑rich, and it aligns with vision‑guided chunking’s insight that respecting document structure yields better RAG performance.[11]

## 4. Query Rewriting, HyDE, And Advanced Retrieval Augmentation

### 4.1 HyDE: Hypothetical Document Embeddings For Zero‑Shot Dense Retrieval

Hypothetical Document Embeddings (HyDE) are a prominent query expansion technique designed to improve zero‑shot dense retrieval by using an LLM to generate synthetic “hypothetical” answers to a query and then embedding these answers to retrieve real documents most similar to the synthetic ones. Gao et al. introduced HyDE as a way to overcome the main challenge in zero‑shot dense retrieval: learning query and document embedding functions that capture relevance without human‑annotated relevance scores.[5] HyDE reframes the task as document‑document similarity rather than query‑document similarity. Given a query \(q\), an LLM \(\textnormal{LLM}_{\textnormal{DocGen}}\) is prompted to generate \(N\) hypothetical documents \(\{\hat{d}_1, \dots, \hat{d}_N\}\) that represent plausible answers or relevant content, such that \(\hat{d}_i = \textnormal{LLM}_{\textnormal{DocGen}}(q)\) for \(1 \leq i \leq N\).[5] These hypothetical documents are then encoded by an unsupervised contrastive encoder \(f\), and their embeddings are averaged with the original query embedding to produce an updated query representation \(\hat{v}_{q_{\textnormal{HyDE}}} = \frac{1}{N+1}\left(f(q) + \sum_{i=1}^N f(\hat{d}_i)\right)\).[5] This enriched query embedding is then used to search against the corpus embeddings, retrieving real documents whose embeddings are closest to \(\hat{v}_{q_{\textnormal{HyDE}}}\).[5] 

HyDE has been shown to outperform state‑of‑the‑art unsupervised dense retrievers, particularly in zero‑shot settings where no relevance supervision is available.[5] By generating hypothetical documents, HyDE effectively expands the query into multiple perspectives and representations, capturing semantic nuances that simple query embeddings miss. In financial RAG, HyDE improves alignment between queries and relevant passages, especially when the original query is underspecified or phrased differently than the source content. For instance, queries that reference financial ratios or vague descriptions of company performance may not directly match the language used in annual reports, but hypothetical documents that express these concepts more fully enable denser embeddings to retrieve the right sections.[4] HyDE thus acts as a bridge between user queries and document semantics, leveraging LLM generation to enrich retrieval signals.[5][4] 

In your competition, HyDE‑style query expansion is attractive when questions are abstract or conceptual, such as “How does the IFMIF‑DONES design handle beam loss mitigation?” or “What are the main limitations of the proposed AI optimization algorithm?” These queries may not contain exact keywords present in the PDFs, making BM25 less effective, while dense embeddings can benefit from synthetic documents that elaborate on the topic. However, implementing HyDE under a “single model” constraint is challenging: HyDE originally assumes a separate LLM for document generation and a contrastive encoder for embedding, both of which may be distinct from your main model.[5] You could adapt HyDE by using Olmo‑3.1 to generate hypothetical passages and then embedding both queries and hypothetical documents with an Olmo‑based embedding prompt, but this would require careful prompt engineering and possibly additional computation per query. Given your one‑hour constraint, full HyDE may be feasible only for a subset of questions, not all.[5][16]

### 4.2 Multi‑HyDE And Multi‑Query Expansion In Financial RAG

A recent extension, Multi‑HyDE, combines multi‑query based retrieval with HyDE to further enhance retrieval effectiveness without increasing token costs over basic HyDE. In the financial RAG framework, Multi‑HyDE generates multiple non‑equivalent hypothetical documents based on diverse variants of the input query, each representing a different perspective or level of detail.[4] For each query \(q\), a generator \(g\) produces \(N\) hypothetical documents \(\hat{d}_i\), and an embedding model \(f\) is used to generate “hypothetical document embeddings” \(\hat{v}\) for each variant, as described above.[4] These embeddings are then used to retrieve real documents from the dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) that are similar to the hypothetical ones, sampling \(N\) documents from the corpus based on similarity.[4] Multi‑HyDE integrates this process with BM25 and a re‑ranking step, forming a unified pipeline that combines dense and sparse retrieval strategies for large, structured financial corpora.[4][10] 

The financial RAG authors highlight that Multi‑HyDE, when combined with BM25 for tables and appropriate rerankers, demonstrates superior performance in capturing relevant information from financial reports, particularly when documents across multiple years have semantically similar content.[4][10] Dense retrieval identifies relevant information across varied sources, while sparse keyword retrieval (BM25) locates structured information such as exact years and financial figures. Hybrid retrieval plus Multi‑HyDE improves coverage and disambiguation, ensuring that the system can handle more cases than any individual method.[4] However, they also note that query expansion methods like HyDE and multi‑query retrieval provide limited benefit for precise numerical queries, where lexical precision and table‑specific retrieval dominate.[10] Multi‑HyDE’s strength lies in cases where queries are underspecified, paraphrased, or conceptually oriented, not in exact numeric lookup tasks.[4][10] 

Translating Multi‑HyDE’s insights to your domain suggests that multi‑query expansion with Olmo could help for conceptual questions and cross‑document synthesis. For example, a question about “beam dynamics in IFMIF‑DONES” could be expanded into multiple synthetic sub‑queries like “linear beam transport in IFMIF,” “space‑charge effects in DONES design,” and “beam loss mitigation strategies,” each generating hypothetical passages whose embeddings guide retrieval.[4] You can then fuse results from these hypothetical expansions using RRF or similar rank fusion, and pass the top chunks to Olmo for generation.[10][19] As in financial RAG, you would combine this with BM25 for precise terms like parameter names, experimental labels, or table captions, ensuring that numeric questions still rely on lexical precision.[4][10] The main limitation, again, is computational: generating multiple hypothetical documents per query with Olmo adds latency and token usage. A selective deployment of Multi‑HyDE—triggered only when initial BM25+dense retrieval shows low confidence, perhaps as measured by low reranker scores or high dispersion in rankings—would be a reasonable compromise.[4][10][16]

### 4.3 Query Rewriting, Summarization‑Based Retrieval, And Title‑Level Routing

Beyond HyDE, the long‑context versus RAG study emphasizes summarization‑based retrieval as a strong competitor to both RAG and long‑context ingestion.[2] Summarization‑based retrieval involves generating summaries of document sections and using these summaries for retrieval instead of the full text, improving context relevance by focusing on main points and reducing noise. The study finds that summarization‑based retrieval performs comparably to long‑context on question‑answering benchmarks, while chunk‑based retrieval lags behind, underscoring the importance of context relevance.[2] This suggests that for your pipeline, summarizing sections or entire documents with Olmo and then indexing these summaries alongside the full text could improve retrieval. Queries could match section titles and summaries, and RAG would then retrieve both the summary and the original section for generation.

MODE’s document‑expert routing inherently uses title‑level and cluster‑level information to route queries; document experts can be represented by centroids that are influenced by document titles, section headers, and global summaries.[14] Combining MODE with summarization‑based retrieval yields a multi‑tier architecture: cluster documents into experts, generate summaries at document and section levels, index both summaries and raw text, and use a hybrid BM25+dense search over summaries for initial routing, followed by RRF over chunks for fine‑grained retrieval. Olmo can generate summaries during preprocessing, using prompts tailored to scientific documents, such as “Summarize the main contributions and experimental setup of this section.” These summaries, when indexed, improve retrieval precision because BM25 can match queries to summary phrases, while dense embeddings capture semantic similarity between query concepts and summary content.[2][14][16]

Title‑level routing is particularly important in mixed‑domain corpora. In your competition, the distinction between accelerator physics and AI papers can be detected from titles and abstracts, allowing you to route queries to the correct domain expert cluster before performing detailed retrieval.[14] This reduces the risk of retrieving AI papers when the question is about neutron sources, or physics reports when the question concerns transformer training regimes. MODE’s centroid matching at the cluster level is effectively a form of title and summary‑based routing: the centroids represent thematic topics across documents, often reflecting the vocabulary of titles and abstracts.[14] Using Olmo to generate improved titles and meta‑descriptions for each document during preprocessing can further enhance this routing, particularly for PDFs with weak or generic titles.

### 4.4 Limits Of Query Expansion For Numeric And Table‑Heavy Questions

The financial RAG and Corrective RAG benchmarks reveal an important limitation of query expansion methods: they provide limited benefit for precise numerical queries and table‑heavy questions.[10][4] In the financial domain, queries often request exact values, such as “What was the company’s net income in 2020?” or “What is the current ratio reported in the latest annual report?” For such questions, lexical precision via BM25 and table‑specific retrieval outperforms dense semantic methods and HyDE, because the relevant passage includes exact numbers and specific phrases that must match the query.[10] Query expansion with HyDE or multi‑query methods can even hurt performance by introducing synthetic documents that emphasize concepts over exact values, leading dense retrieval to select semantically similar but numerically incorrect sections.[10][4] 

Given that accelerator physics reports and AI papers also contain many tables and numerical specifications—beam currents, energy levels, hyperparameters, performance metrics—your pipeline must be careful not to over‑apply query expansion to numeric questions. Instead, you should identify queries that are primarily numeric or table‑oriented, such as those mentioning “value,” “ratio,” “error,” “accuracy,” or specific units (e.g., MeV, A, s), and route them to a table‑aware retrieval path using BM25 over table captions and headers.[4][10] Dense retrieval can still play a role in identifying semantically relevant tables, but HyDE and multi‑query expansion should be avoided or used with caution. Corrective RAG’s experiments show that query expansion methods like HyDE and multi‑query provide limited benefit for precise numerical queries, reinforcing this caution.[10] 

To implement this selective approach, you can design a simple classifier—either rule‑based or Olmo‑based—that inspects queries and flags those that are numeric or table‑centric. For flagged queries, you prioritize BM25 retrieval over table headers and section titles, possibly followed by a lightweight reranking step using Olmo to check whether the retrieved chunk contains a table that matches the query topic.[10][4][16] HyDE or summarization‑based retrieval can be reserved for conceptual or descriptive questions, where synthetic documents help enrich the query representation. This differentiation ensures that query expansion contributes to accuracy gains where it is most effective, while avoiding pitfalls in numeric QA, in line with the financial RAG findings.[10][4]

## 5. Reranking, Self‑Reflection, And Self‑Consistency For Scientific QA

### 5.1 Cross‑Encoder Rerankers: BGE, Jina, BCE, And MXBAI

Reranking—the process of scoring and reordering a candidate set of retrieved documents—is one of the most powerful levers in RAG systems. SciRerankBench, a benchmark designed to evaluate rerankers towards scientific search, categorizes rerankers in RAG‑LLMs into several architectures and shows that dense cross‑encoder rerankers outperform other types on semantically challenging tasks.[6] Cross‑encoder rerankers like BGE, Jina, BCE (NetEase Youdao), and MXBAI compute contextualized relevance scores via bi‑sequence encoders: they jointly encode the query and document passage, allowing the model to capture fine‑grained interactions between tokens in the query and passage.[6] This stands in contrast to bi‑encoder or dual‑encoder models, which encode queries and documents separately into vectors and rely on simple similarity measures. In SciRerankBench, cross‑encoders consistently achieve higher ranking quality on tasks involving nuanced scientific semantics, such as distinguishing between subtly different research questions or identifying the most relevant paper in a set of conceptually related articles.[6] 

Although the excerpt does not provide numerical metrics, the qualitative takeaway is clear: cross‑encoders are the gold standard for reranking in scientific domains, and their products have received significant attention from academia and industry.[6] However, deploying separate cross‑encoder models like BGE or Jina may violate your competition’s “single model” constraint, and loading a large cross‑encoder on a single H100 alongside Olmo‑3.1‑32B could be infeasible within the one‑hour time limit.[16] To approximate cross‑encoder reranking under these constraints, you can use Olmo itself as a reranker via carefully designed prompts. For each candidate passage retrieved by BM25+dense, you can prompt Olmo with the query and the passage and ask it to output a relevance score or a categorical judgment (e.g., “highly relevant,” “partially relevant,” “irrelevant”). This approach effectively turns Olmo into a cross‑encoder reranker: it processes the query and passage jointly and produces a relevance assessment.[6][16] 

This Olmo‑based reranking will be slower than embedding‑based scoring but should be applied only to a small candidate set (for example, the top 20 or 30 passages from hybrid retrieval), limiting computational overhead. The financial RAG benchmark shows that adding a neural reranker (Cohere Rerank) on top of hybrid retrieval yields substantial improvements: Recall@5 increases from about 0.695 for hybrid RRF alone to approximately 0.816 for hybrid plus rerank, and MRR@3 rises from 0.433 to about 0.605.[10] If Olmo can approximate Cohere’s reranking quality, similar gains may be achievable in your domain. SciRerankBench’s finding that cross‑encoders outperform other rerankers suggests that investing resources in reranking is worthwhile, even if implemented via prompts rather than separate models.[6]

### 5.2 Self‑RAG: Learning To Retrieve, Generate, And Critique Through Self‑Reflection

Self‑RAG introduces a self‑reflective framework that enhances an LM’s quality and factuality through retrieval and self‑reflection, without sacrificing creativity and versatility. Asai et al. propose training a single arbitrary LM to adaptively retrieve passages on demand, generate answers, and reflect on both retrieved passages and its own generations using special reflection tokens.[12] The Self‑RAG model predicts “reflection tokens” as part of its output, which signal when retrieval is needed, when a retrieved passage should be critiqued for relevance, and when an answer segment should be evaluated for support by the passage.[12] During inference, if retrieval is not required, the model continues generating text as a standard LM. If retrieval is needed, it generates a critique token to evaluate the retrieved passage’s relevance, then the next response segment, and then another critique token to assess whether the information in that segment is supported by the passage.[12] This unified approach trains the LM to retrieve, generate, and critique via next‑token prediction, adding reflection tokens to the vocabulary and tuning the model accordingly.[12] 

Experiments show that Self‑RAG, with model sizes of 7B and 13B parameters, significantly outperforms state‑of‑the‑art LLMs and retrieval‑augmented models on a diverse set of tasks, including open‑domain QA, reasoning, and fact verification.[12] Specifically, Self‑RAG outperforms ChatGPT and retrieval‑augmented Llama2‑chat on factuality and citation accuracy for long‑form generations, demonstrating that self‑reflection and critique lead to more grounded answers.[12] The framework thus offers a way to integrate retrieval and verification within a single model, making retrieval adaptive and controllable during inference. For your competition, you cannot retrain Olmo with reflection tokens, but you can approximate Self‑RAG behavior via multi‑step prompting. For instance, after retrieving passages, you can ask Olmo to generate an answer and then separately prompt it to critique the answer’s support in the retrieved passages, possibly revising the answer if inconsistencies are detected.[12][16] 

A simple implementation might involve three steps per question: first, retrieve and rerank passages; second, generate an initial answer with citations; third, prompt Olmo to reflect on whether each cited passage supports the corresponding claim, producing a corrected answer if necessary. This manual Self‑RAG can improve factuality, aligning with the framework’s findings that reflection tokens and critique improve grounding and citation accuracy.[12] It also harmonizes with the LLM judge evaluation: answers that explicitly reference retrieved evidence and acknowledge uncertainty or limitations are more likely to be judged correct than unqualified assertions, especially when the judge uses similar reflection‑based scoring heuristics.[12]

### 5.3 Self‑Consistency Improves Chain‑Of‑Thought Reasoning

Self‑consistency is a decoding strategy designed to enhance chain‑of‑thought (CoT) reasoning in language models. Wang et al. propose replacing naive greedy decoding with self‑consistency: instead of generating a single reasoning path, the model samples a diverse set of reasoning paths for a given problem and then selects the most consistent answer by marginalizing over these paths.[18] The intuition is that complex reasoning problems often admit multiple ways of thinking that lead to the same correct answer; by sampling and aggregating, the model can identify answers that are supported by multiple independent chains, reducing idiosyncratic errors.[18] 

Extensive empirical evaluation shows that self‑consistency boosts CoT performance with striking margins on arithmetic and commonsense reasoning benchmarks. On GSM8K, self‑consistency improves accuracy by about 17.9 percentage points; on SVAMP, by roughly 11.0 points; on AQuA, by approximately 12.2 points; on StrategyQA, by about 6.4 points; and on ARC‑Challenge, by around 3.9 points.[18] These gains are achieved without modifying the underlying model, relying solely on a smarter decoding strategy. Although these benchmarks involve math and logic rather than document QA, the principle applies to your competition: questions about accelerator physics and AI research may require multi‑step reasoning based on retrieved evidence, and sampling multiple reasoning paths can help verify conclusions.

In practice, self‑consistency can be adapted to RAG by sampling multiple answer chains that use the same retrieved passages but different reasoning prompts. For example, after retrieving relevant sections about beam design, you can prompt Olmo to answer the question with chain‑of‑thought reasoning three to five times, each time with a slightly different prompt (“Explain step by step,” “Reason through the design rationale,” “Provide a detailed argument.”). You then aggregate the final answers and select the one that appears most frequently or is most consistent across samples, possibly using Olmo or a simple heuristic to assess consistency.[18][16] This approach is computationally expensive, as it requires multiple generations per question, but can be reserved for high‑stakes questions or those where initial answers are uncertain. Given your one‑hour limit, you might limit self‑consistency sampling to one or two extra chains per question, which still yields some robustness without excessive cost.

Self‑consistency also complements Self‑RAG‑style reflection. After selecting a consensus answer, you can prompt Olmo to critique the answer against the retrieved passages, as described above, further improving factuality and alignment with evidence.[12][18] Together, these techniques form a strong verification pipeline: cross‑encoder‑style reranking narrows down relevant passages; self‑consistency sampling explores multiple reasoning paths; and self‑reflection critiques and corrects answers. This pipeline is likely to produce answers that both match the ground truth and satisfy an LLM judge’s expectations for reasoning and supporting evidence.[6][12][18]

### 5.4 LLM Judges, Citation Accuracy, And Answer Presentation

LLM‑based judges evaluate answers based on correctness, coherence, and grounding, often using internal heuristics that favor structured, well‑explained responses with explicit references to evidence. Self‑RAG’s experiments show that adding reflection tokens and training the model to critique its own predictions results in significant gains in factuality and citation accuracy for long‑form generations compared to ChatGPT and retrieval‑augmented Llama2‑chat.[12] In particular, the model learns to highlight which passages support which claims, and to flag unsupported assertions, thereby producing answers that are easier for judges to score accurately.[12] Environmental QA and financial RAG studies also emphasize citation and grounding: models that explicitly reference retrieved passages or tables are favored in evaluation.[7][4][10] 

For your competition, answer presentation should therefore include clear citations to specific sections or tables, ideally with identifiers that match how the corpus is indexed (e.g., document titles, section headings, table numbers). When retrieving chunks with contextual headers, you should retain these headers in the prompt so that Olmo can use them in its citations. For example, an answer might say, “According to the ‘Beam Design’ section of the IFMIF‑DONES design report, page 35, the beam current is specified as 125 mA,” referencing the chunk header and page number. This style aligns with Self‑RAG’s focus on citation accuracy and improves the judge’s ability to verify correctness.[12][7] If you track chunk IDs and parent document IDs, as recommended by the parent‑document retriever analysis, you can use these IDs in citations, further enhancing traceability.[17] 

In addition, self‑consistency and self‑reflection can be included in answer presentation. You might append a short “validation” paragraph where Olmo briefly explains how it checked the answer against retrieved passages, akin to a reflection token expanded into natural language, such as “The above answer was derived by comparing sections X and Y and verifying that the reported value is consistent across them.” This meta‑reasoning both improves correctness and signals robustness to the judge.[12][18] Overall, retrieval quality, reranking, and verification strategies must be designed with answer presentation in mind: the same techniques that improve factuality also make it easier for an LLM judge to recognize a correct answer.

## 6. Tables, Numeric QA, And Structured Data Handling

### 6.1 Table‑Specific Retrieval And Lessons From Financial RAG

Scientific PDFs, like financial reports, often contain tables that summarize critical numerical information. In financial RAG with Multi‑HyDE, the authors explicitly integrate a keyword‑based retriever like BM25 for structured data such as tables, recognizing that dense retrieval alone struggles with table structure and exact term matching.[4] Their framework combines multi‑query based HyDE with BM25 in a unified pipeline optimized for financial documents, where BM25 handles tables and sparse keyword matching, while dense retrieval captures semantic connections across text sections.[4][10] They show that hybrid retrieval with BM25 clearly outperforms dense retrieval methods for long‑document financial data, and that pairing dense retrieval with sparse keyword‑based retrieval improves overall performance by handling more cases than any individual method.[4][10] 

Corrective RAG further demonstrates that BM25 outperforms state‑of‑the‑art dense retrieval on financial documents and that hybrid retrieval fused with RRF plus neural reranking yields the best retrieval performance.[10] Query expansion methods like HyDE and multi‑query provide limited benefit for precise numerical queries, as discussed earlier, underscoring the need for table‑specific retrieval.[10] The authors attribute improved performance to the fact that financial reports across multiple years have semantically similar content, and that pairing dense retrieval with BM25 enables the system to identify relevant information across a variety of potential sources while maintaining lexical precision for structured information.[4][10] 

For accelerator physics and AI papers, similar patterns hold. Tables may list beam parameters, energy spectra, error budgets, experimental conditions, training hyperparameters, model sizes, and evaluation metrics. Queries that ask for exact values or comparisons across these parameters should be routed to a table‑aware retrieval path. You can extract tables from PDFs using preprocessing tools and associate them with textual headers and summaries. BM25 can then match queries to table captions and headers, while dense retrieval can identify semantically related tables based on their summaries.[4][10] Once relevant tables are retrieved, Olmo can answer numeric questions by reading table cells within short chunks, minimizing the risk of hallucination. This approach leverages the financial RAG insight that BM25 and table‑specific retrieval are crucial for numeric QA.[4][10]

### 6.2 Table Extraction, Alignment, And Chunk Design

Implementing table‑aware retrieval requires careful chunk design. Tables should be treated as distinct chunks, with text that includes the table caption, column headers, and row labels, as well as a human‑readable representation of key values. For example, a table chunk might be represented as a text block that lists column names followed by row entries in a structured format, ensuring that BM25 and dense embeddings can capture both labels and values.[4][10] In many cases, you can also generate a short summary of the table using Olmo, describing the main trends or key values in natural language. This summary can be used as an additional retrieval signal and as an aid for answering questions. Summarization‑based retrieval, as highlighted in the long‑context versus RAG study, performs comparably to long‑context, suggesting that table summaries can be powerful for retrieval.[2] 

Alignment between queries and tables is critical. Queries may reference table values indirectly, such as “What is the nominal beam current specified for the IFMIF‑DONES accelerator?” or “How does the error rate change with dataset size in the AI experiment?” These queries may not mention the table caption exactly, but their semantics align with the table content. Dense embeddings of table summaries can capture this alignment, while BM25 ensures that exact terms like “beam current” or “error rate” match table headers.[4][10] Hybrid retrieval over table chunks, with RRF fusion and Olmo‑based reranking, can thus identify the correct tables within the corpus. Once retrieved, tables must be presented to Olmo in a format it can parse; simple text representations with clear delimiters for rows and columns work, especially when accompanied by explanatory headers.[4][10][16] 

To avoid parent‑document retriever issues, you should not feed entire sections containing many tables; instead, retrieve only the specific table chunks and their immediate context. FutureAGI’s analysis of parent‑document retrievers emphasizes measuring ChunkUtilization to flag parents whose retrieved text is mostly unused.[17] Tables that are not referenced in the answer should not appear in the prompt, as they can distract Olmo and lead to confusion. Metrics like ChunkAttribution and ContextRelevance can help you tune the chunking and retrieval pipeline to include only tables that actually contribute to answers, improving both accuracy and efficiency.[17]

### 6.3 Numeric QA Performance, Error Modes, And Mitigation

Numeric QA has distinct error modes compared to textual QA. Models may misread table entries, misalign units, confuse years or versions, or interpolate values incorrectly. Financial RAG’s finding that BM25 outperforms dense retrieval on text‑and‑table corpora suggests that lexically precise retrieval mitigates some of these errors, but generation still poses risks.[10] Self‑RAG and self‑consistency can help reduce numeric errors by adding verification steps. For instance, after retrieving a table and generating an answer, you can prompt Olmo to double‑check the value against the table text, explicitly asking, “Verify that the value X matches the entry in the table labeled Y.” This self‑reflection may catch mistakes where Olmo misread a row or column.[12][18] 

In addition, you can design specialized prompts for numeric QA that encourage careful reading, such as “Extract the exact numerical value from the following table that answers the question. Do not approximate.” This reduces the likelihood of approximations or interpolations. Self‑consistency can be applied by sampling multiple readings of the same table and comparing the answers; if they disagree, you can trigger a more careful verification procedure.[18][16] These techniques align with the broader theme that verification strategies improve factuality and citation accuracy, as demonstrated in Self‑RAG.[12] Considering that numeric questions are likely to be scored strictly by the LLM judge, investing in specialized numeric QA prompts and verification is justified.

## 7. Computational Intelligence And Evolutionary Prompt Optimization For RAG

### 7.1 EvoPrompt: Evolutionary Algorithms For Discrete Prompt Optimization

Automatic prompt optimization has emerged as a powerful way to enhance LLM performance without gradient updates. EvoPrompt introduces a novel framework for discrete prompt optimization that borrows ideas from evolutionary algorithms (EAs), which exhibit good performance and fast convergence.[1] EvoPrompt aims to optimize natural language prompts, which are discrete and need to be coherent and human‑readable, by connecting LLMs with evolutionary algorithms in a gradient‑free manner.[1] The framework starts from a population of prompts and iteratively generates new prompts with LLMs based on evolutionary operators such as selection, mutation, and crossover, improving the population based on a development set.[1] 

In particular, EvoPrompt abstains from gradients or parameter updates, relying entirely on prompt evolution driven by LLM sampling. It optimizes prompts for both closed‑ and open‑source LLMs, including GPT‑3.5 and Alpaca, across 31 datasets covering language understanding, generation tasks, and BIG‑Bench Hard tasks.[1] The authors report that EvoPrompt significantly outperforms human‑engineered prompts and existing automatic prompt generation methods, achieving up to 25% accuracy improvement on BBH tasks and substantial gains across other benchmarks.[1] A later survey of automatic prompt optimization with instruction‑focused LLMs notes that evolutionary algorithms are widely utilized in this context, highlighting genetic algorithms (GA) and differential evolution (DE) as key variants.[8] GA applies mutation, selection, and crossover to iteratively enhance prompts, while DE generates new prompt candidates by using differences between existing solutions, promoting diversity while converging toward optimal solutions.[8] EvoPrompt systematically compares GA and DE across identical tasks, demonstrating that each algorithm excels in different scenarios.[8] 

For your competition, EvoPrompt’s insights suggest that you can treat the RAG pipeline and its prompts as optimization targets. You can define a population of RAG prompts that control how Olmo interprets retrieved passages, how it cites evidence, and how it performs self‑reflection. Using a small development set of practice questions, you can run an evolutionary loop: evaluate each prompt by its accuracy on the dev set, select the best, mutate them (e.g., rephrasing instructions about citation or reasoning), and crossover elements across prompts to generate new candidates.[1][8] Olmo itself can be used to propose mutations and crossovers, as in EvoPrompt’s integration of LLMs with EAs.[1] Given your one‑hour evaluation time, prompt optimization must be done before the competition, but the resulting optimized prompts can then be used during the competition to maximize accuracy.

### 7.2 Promptbreeder: Self‑Referential Self‑Improvement Via Prompt Evolution

Promptbreeder introduces a self‑referential self‑improvement mechanism that evolves and adapts prompts for a given domain by using an LLM to generate and improve mutation prompts for task prompts.[13] Fernando et al. note that popular prompt strategies like chain‑of‑thought prompting can dramatically improve reasoning abilities but are often sub‑optimal and hand‑crafted.[13] Promptbreeder addresses this by maintaining populations of task prompts and mutation prompts. The LLM mutates task prompts using mutation prompts, evaluates them on a training set, and then improves the mutation prompts themselves based on their effectiveness, creating a self‑referential loop where prompts that generate better mutations are favored.[13] 

Crucially, Promptbreeder is not just improving task prompts but also the mutation prompts that generate these improvements. This self‑referential mechanism allows the system to discover intricate task‑specific prompts that outperform standard CoT and Plan‑and‑Solve prompting on arithmetic and commonsense reasoning benchmarks.[13] Promptbreeder has been shown to evolve prompts that handle hate speech classification and other challenging tasks, highlighting its generality.[13] A recent multimodal extension, “Evolutionary Prompt Optimization Discovers Emergent Multimodal Reasoning Strategies in Vision‑Language Models,” notes that methods like Promptbreeder and evolutionary algorithms such as EvoPrompt have shown promise in text‑only scenarios, and investigates their application to vision‑language tasks.[15] This extension finds that evolutionary prompt optimization can discover emergent multimodal reasoning strategies, reinforcing the effectiveness of these techniques beyond pure text.[15] 

In your context, Promptbreeder’s self‑referential evolution can be applied to RAG orchestration prompts. You can define task prompts that instruct Olmo how to use retrieved passages, how to cite evidence, and how to perform self‑reflection. Mutation prompts can describe ways to tweak these task prompts—e.g., “add more emphasis on citations,” “encourage step‑by‑step reasoning,” “request explicit mention of table headers.” Olmo can generate new task prompts based on mutation prompts, and you can evaluate them on a dev set of questions. Over multiple generations, you can evolve prompts that produce higher accuracy and better citation behavior, similar to Promptbreeder’s improvements on reasoning benchmarks.[13][15] This process leverages Olmo’s tool‑use and instruction‑following capabilities, as described in its documentation, to optimize prompts for your specific competition.[16]

### 7.3 Applying Evolutionary Prompt Optimization To RAG Pipelines

Integrating EvoPrompt and Promptbreeder‑style evolution into a RAG pipeline involves several design choices. First, you must decide which prompts to optimize. Candidates include retrieval prompts (instructions for query rewriting or HyDE‑like synthetic document generation), reranking prompts (instructions for Olmo when scoring passage relevance), generation prompts (instructions for answering questions and citing evidence), and verification prompts (instructions for self‑reflection and self‑consistency). Each of these prompts influences different parts of the pipeline: retrieval quality, ranking quality, reasoning robustness, and citation accuracy.[1][13][8] 

Second, you need a development set of questions and ground‑truth answers, ideally representative of the competition’s domains (accelerator physics and AI research). You can construct such a set by sampling public reports and papers related to IFMIF‑DONES and AI, and manually or semi‑automatically creating expert questions with known answers. Evaluation metrics could mimic the competition’s LLM judge, using Olmo or another model to score correctness and grounding, or using automatic metrics like exact match and F1 for numerical and factual questions.[12][7][10] EvoPrompt’s framework suggests using performance on the development set as the fitness function in the evolutionary algorithm, selecting prompts that yield higher accuracy.[1] Promptbreeder’s self‑referential design suggests also optimizing mutation prompts, ensuring that the search process itself improves over time.[13]

Third, you must implement evolutionary operators. For discrete prompts, mutation can involve adding, deleting, or rephrasing instructions, changing examples, or altering the tone. Crossover can combine segments from two prompts, such as merging the citation instructions from one with the reasoning instructions from another.[1][8][13] Olmo can assist in generating mutated prompts when given appropriate meta‑prompts, such as “Given this prompt, propose three variants that emphasize citation accuracy,” aligning with Promptbreeder’s use of LLMs to generate mutation prompts.[13][15] The search space is large, but evolutionary algorithms are designed to explore it efficiently, converging toward high‑performing prompts over successive generations.[1][8]

By applying evolutionary prompt optimization to your RAG pipeline before the competition, you can discover prompts that synergize with hybrid retrieval, reranking, self‑consistency, and self‑reflection, yielding higher overall accuracy. The literature reports double‑digit percentage gains in reasoning tasks using these methods, suggesting that similar magnitude improvements may be achievable in scientific QA, especially if prompts are tuned to your specific corpus and question styles.[1][13][15]

### 7.4 Rank Fusion And Fuzzy Aggregation Ideas

Computational intelligence also offers ideas for rank fusion and fuzzy aggregation of retrieval signals. While the financial RAG benchmark uses Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) as a simple but effective unsupervised method to combine BM25 and dense retrieval ranks, broader IR literature explores fuzzy rank fusion, where ranks and scores are combined using fuzzy logic to capture uncertainty and partial relevance.[10][19] Ma et al. show that sparse–dense hybrid search significantly improves DPR’s retrieval accuracy, and their fusion rule, though not explicitly fuzzy, effectively weights contributions from BM25 and DPR based on rank positions.[19] 

In your pipeline, you can extend RRF with fuzzy weighting based on reranker scores or Olmo‑based relevance judgments. For example, you might assign fuzzy membership degrees to each passage indicating its relevance category (high, medium, low) based on Olmo’s reranking, and then combine these degrees with BM25 and dense ranks in a fuzzy fusion framework. While detailed fuzzy rank fusion methods are beyond the scope of the provided excerpts, the general idea is that computational intelligence techniques can help integrate multiple signals—lexical scores, dense similarity, reranker judgments—into a unified relevance estimate that better reflects the nuanced nature of scientific QA.[10][19] 

This fusion can be tuned using evolutionary algorithms as well. You can define parameters for weighting BM25, dense, and reranker contributions, and evolve these parameters using a development set and an evolutionary search, similar to EvoPrompt but applied to numerical fusion parameters rather than prompts.[1][8] The result is a hybrid retrieval and reranking system whose fusion strategy is optimized for your corpus and question styles, potentially yielding further gains over standard RRF. Given that hybrid retrieval already improves recall and MRR by 15–30% over single‑method pipelines, optimized fusion might yield additional percentage points in accuracy, which can be decisive in a competition evaluated by an LLM judge.[10][19]

## 8. Integrative Design Recommendations For The Competition System

### 8.1 End‑To‑End Architecture Suggestion Under Constraints

Drawing together the insights from hybrid retrieval, reranking, chunking, query expansion, verification, and prompt optimization, an end‑to‑end architecture for your competition system can be sketched as follows. Preprocessing transforms the blind corpus of scientific PDFs into structured chunks: sections, tables, figures, and equations, each with contextual headers including document title, section title, and a short summary generated by Olmo.[11][2][16] Tables are extracted into text chunks with captions and headers, and document‑level summaries are produced for title‑level routing.[4][10][2] Documents are embedded using Olmo‑based embedding prompts, and clustered into document experts using MODE‑style centroid clustering, forming physics and AI clusters and possibly sub‑clusters by topic.[14][16] BM25 indices are built over chunk text and headers, while dense embeddings are stored in FAISS indices, possibly compressed following sparse–dense hybrid search practices.[19][10] 

At inference time, for each question, the system first performs document‑expert routing: using BM25 over titles and summaries and dense similarity to cluster centroids, it selects the top one or two clusters.[14][2] Within these clusters, hybrid retrieval is performed: BM25 and dense search generate ranked lists of chunks, which are fused using RRF.[10][19] A small candidate set (e.g., top 20 chunks) is passed to Olmo for cross‑encoder‑style reranking via prompts that jointly present the query and each chunk and ask for a relevance judgment.[6][16] The top few chunks, including table chunks if the query is numeric, are selected as context.[4][10] 

Depending on the query type, the system then optionally performs query expansion or self‑consistency. For conceptual questions or low‑confidence initial retrievals, Olmo generates synthetic hypothetical passages in HyDE or Multi‑HyDE style and uses them to enrich query embeddings for a second retrieval round, fused with the first using rank fusion.[5][4][10] For high‑stakes reasoning questions, Olmo does chain‑of‑thought generation and self‑consistency sampling, producing multiple reasoning paths and aggregating answers.[18][16] For numeric and table‑centric questions, the system avoids query expansion and focuses on BM25 and table‑specific retrieval.[10][4] 

Finally, Olmo generates the answer with explicit citations to chunk headers and document titles, and a brief self‑reflection paragraph describing how it checked the answer against retrieved passages.[12][7][17] This answer presentation is tuned via evolutionary prompt optimization: prompts that instruct Olmo on citation style, reasoning steps, and self‑reflection are optimized using EvoPrompt and Promptbreeder‑style evolution on a development set before the competition.[1][13][8][15] The overall architecture stays within the single‑model constraint by using Olmo for generation, embeddings, reranking, query expansion, and reflection, and BM25 for sparse retrieval, with all neural components running on the H100 and all IR components running on CPU.[10][19][16]

### 8.2 Practical Parameter Choices For Single H100 And One Hour

Within the one‑hour evaluation limit, parameter choices must balance accuracy and latency. Hybrid retrieval and BM25 can be made fast by precomputing indices and using optimized libraries, while dense retrieval using Olmo‑generated embeddings must be limited to small batches to avoid GPU bottlenecks.[10][19][16] You might restrict dense retrieval to cluster‑level or summary‑level search, reducing the number of embeddings. Reranking via Olmo prompts should be applied to a small candidate set (e.g., 10–20 chunks), with short prompts to minimize tokens.[6][16] Self‑consistency sampling can be limited to two to three additional chains for the most difficult questions, and HyDE‑style query expansion can be triggered only when initial retrieval confidence is low, as measured by reranker scores or rank dispersion.[5][4][10] 

Chunk sizes should be chosen to fit multiple chunks into Olmo’s context window along with the question and answer, likely on the order of a few hundred tokens per chunk.[11][2][16] Table chunks must be small enough that the entire table can be processed in one pass. Context window usage should be monitored to avoid exceeding limits, and parent‑document retriever patterns must be avoided by returning only child chunks with contextual headers.[17][10] Preprocessing and evolutionary prompt optimization should be completed before the competition, so that the runtime system focuses on retrieval and generation, not prompt search.[1][13][8][15] Logging retrieval spans and answer citations will help debug and refine the system during practice runs.

### 8.3 Evaluation, Ablation, And Fallback Strategies

Before the competition, you should evaluate and ablate your system components on a development set, measuring accuracy and latency. Ablation studies can compare BM25‑only retrieval, dense‑only retrieval, hybrid RRF, and hybrid+reranking, mirroring the financial RAG benchmark where hybrid+rerank achieved Recall@5 around 0.816 and MRR@3 around 0.605, outperforming BM25 and dense alone.[10] You can also compare chunking strategies: fixed‑length chunks versus section‑based chunks with contextual headers, drawing inspiration from vision‑guided chunking and contextual retrieval.[11][10] Long‑context baselines—feeding entire sections or documents to Olmo—can be tested on a subset of questions to assess whether any accuracy gains justify the token cost, referencing long‑context versus RAG findings.[2][9][7] 

Fallback strategies are important. If hybrid retrieval or reranking fails (e.g., no relevant chunks are found or reranker scores are low), you can fall back to BM25‑only retrieval and zero‑shot answering, or to long‑context ingestion of the most relevant document summary.[10][2] Self‑consistency and self‑reflection can be disabled for low‑stakes questions or when time is short. Evolutionary prompt optimization can provide multiple prompt variants; if one prompt leads to poor performance on a particular question type, you can fallback to a more conservative prompt that emphasizes direct citation and minimal reasoning.[1][13][8][15] This adaptive design ensures that your system remains robust under varying conditions and aligns with the competition’s scoring by an LLM judge.

## Conclusion

Recent research between 2023 and 2026 on retrieval‑augmented generation, long‑context processing, automatic prompt optimization, and verification offers clear guidance for building a high‑accuracy system to answer expert questions over a blind corpus of scientific PDFs using Olmo‑3.1‑32B‑Instruct on a single H100. Hybrid sparse–dense retrieval with BM25 and dense embeddings, fused via Reciprocal Rank Fusion and followed by neural reranking, delivers the largest measured retrieval accuracy gains on long, technical corpora, as demonstrated by the financial RAG benchmark’s Recall@5 around 0.816 and MRR@3 about 0.605 for hybrid+rerank compared to lower scores for BM25 and dense alone.[10][19] Document‑expert routing via MODE, contextual chunking with section and table‑aware headers, and avoidance of parent‑document retriever patterns further enhance retrieval precision and context relevance, addressing the challenges of mixed‑domain corpora with distractors.[14][11][17] Long‑context versus RAG studies clarify that while long‑context models can surpass RAG on some Wikipedia‑style QA benchmarks when sufficiently resourced, RAG remains superior for complex policy and scientific documents under practical constraints, aligning with environmental QA findings.[2][7][9] 

Query expansion techniques like HyDE and Multi‑HyDE improve zero‑shot dense retrieval for underspecified or conceptually oriented queries, especially when coupled with hybrid retrieval and BM25, although their benefits are limited for precise numerical questions where table‑specific retrieval and lexical precision dominate.[5][4][10] Cross‑encoder rerankers such as BGE and Jina are the strongest rerankers for scientific search, and while separate models may be unavailable in your competition, Olmo can approximate cross‑encoder reranking via prompts.[6][16] Self‑RAG and self‑consistency provide powerful verification mechanisms: Self‑RAG improves factuality and citation accuracy through reflection tokens and critique, while self‑consistency boosts chain‑of‑thought reasoning accuracy by 3–18 percentage points across math and commonsense benchmarks.[12][18] These techniques can be adapted to Olmo via multi‑step prompting and sampling, enhancing answer robustness under LLM judgment.

On the prompt side, evolutionary optimization frameworks like EvoPrompt and Promptbreeder demonstrate that gradient‑free prompt evolution, using genetic algorithms and self‑referential mutation prompts, can yield large performance gains on reasoning and classification tasks, and recent multimodal extensions confirm that similar strategies discover emergent reasoning behaviors in vision‑language models.[1][13][8][15] Applying these methods to your RAG pipeline enables optimization of retrieval, reranking, generation, and verification prompts on a development set, tuning the system to your specific corpus and question styles. Rank fusion and fuzzy aggregation ideas from computational intelligence further refine the combination of BM25, dense, and reranker scores, potentially yielding additional improvements over standard RRF.[10][19]

Integrating these insights suggests a concrete architecture for your competition: preprocess PDFs into structured, contextualized chunks; build BM25 and Olmo‑based dense indices; cluster documents into MODE‑style experts; perform hybrid retrieval with RRF and Olmo‑based reranking; selectively apply HyDE and multi‑query expansion; design table‑aware retrieval paths for numeric questions; use self‑consistency and self‑reflection for difficult reasoning tasks; and optimize prompts with evolutionary algorithms before the competition.[4][10][11][14][12][18][1][13][15][16] Such a system maximizes accuracy under the constraints of a single H100 and one‑hour evaluation, leverages Olmo’s strengths in instruction following and tool use, and aligns answer presentation with the expectations of an LLM judge. By grounding every major design choice in recent primary literature and benchmark results, you can prioritize the techniques that offer the largest accuracy gains and avoid known pitfalls, positioning your team to perform strongly in this challenging scientific QA competition.
